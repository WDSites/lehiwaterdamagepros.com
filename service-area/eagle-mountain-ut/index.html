A City Built in a Generation

Eagle Mountain was incorporated in 1996 with about 250 people. The Census Bureau puts the July 2025 estimate at 66,557, which is 52.6 percent above the 2020 base. It is the largest city in Utah County by land area.

Fast growth means newer homes, and newer homes fail in different places than older ones. Common points are appliance hoses, water heater connections, plastic fittings and the first hard freeze in a house that sat empty.

No Secondary Water: Most Leaks Are House Water

Eagle Mountain's state water plan says the city has no secondary irrigation system today and plans one. That means most sprinkler lines in town run on the same culinary supply as the house. A broken sprinkler line is potable water, which is a Category 1 loss if it is caught quickly.

The city says over 75 percent of its water goes to residential use, and 90 percent of that goes to landscape irrigation. Large lawns and long sprinkler lines raise the chance of a break near a foundation.

Sewer Laterals: Whose Line Is It?

The city's page says it maintains collection mains and that the owner is responsible for the service line from the building to the right-of-way. When a sewer backs up into a basement, who fixes the line depends on where the blockage is.

Cleanup is the same either way: contain, remove porous materials, disinfect and dry. See flood damage restoration for Category 3 steps, and call your plumber or the city for the line.

Basement Finishes, Permits and Inspections

The Eagle Mountain Building Department takes permits through an online portal and schedules inspections by email. The page lists permits for decks, retaining walls and pools. After a flood, ask the department whether your repair needs a permit.

A basement that was finished by a builder may have moisture trapped behind new drywall. A moisture survey tells you more than a visual check.
