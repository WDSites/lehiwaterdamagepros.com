Water Damage Restoration in Downtown Lehi, UT

Downtown Lehi is the original town center around Main Street and Center Street. Older masonry, mixed uses and the nearby Dry Creek corridor make water damage here different from a newer subdivision. This page explains what we could verify, what to do first and how drying works in older buildings.

Where Downtown Lehi Is

The National Register nomination for the Lehi Main Street Historic District places it along Main Street from Center Street to 200 West, with Lehi City Hall at 51 N. Center nearby. The nomination says its 28 buildings are all commercial, so the historic district itself is business property.

Downtown Lehi as a neighborhood is broader and mixed. We use Main Street and Center Street as landmarks and do not claim official residential boundaries.

Documented Water History Near Dry Creek

Lehi Free Press reported in 2019 that storm wind and rain filled Dry Creek with debris on about May 15 while crews were clearing screens, and that the creek overflowed behind Lehi Elementary and flooded several homes. City officials called it a perfect storm. The 2023 coverage names Center Street and 100 West near the school.

Residents quoted in those articles reported losses, including one family with about $10,000 in uninsured belongings and another with about three feet of water in a family room. Those are resident accounts and not verified findings. The paper also reports that the city later bought one severely damaged house and rebuilt culverts at 5th West and 2nd West.

A 1983 runoff flood sent water through Dry Creek, and a historical marker stands at 89 W. 500 North. Lehi's 2014 storm drain plan calls Dry Creek the only FEMA-studied channel in the city. Neighborhood-specific FEMA flood-zone status not verified, and we do not say any street is in a zone.

Source types: National Register nomination, Lehi Free Press news reports from 2019, 2023 and 2025, and the Lehi storm drain master plan from 2014.

What Older Buildings Change About Drying

The nomination describes brick and wood buildings on stone foundations, built mostly between 1891 and 1934. Masonry, old plaster and stone hold water and release it slowly, so a drying plan can run longer than for newer drywall.

The published ANSI/IICRC S500 standard is a common reference for drying goals, and we name it as a reference only. We cannot verify what the homes around the district are built from or whether they have basements. If you live or work in an older building, tell us on the first call so the crew can plan for it.

Safety Steps Before You Touch Anything

  1. Do not enter water that could be touching outlets, cords, a furnace or the panel.
  2. If creek or storm water is in the building, treat it as contaminated and keep children and pets out.
  3. Shut off the supply only if you can reach the valve from dry ground.
  4. Take photos of the water line and the damage when it is safe.
  5. Older buildings may contain hazardous materials. Do not scrape or sand wet plaster, paint or flooring. Ask a professional first.

Utah's Division of Air Quality says its asbestos rules cover commercial, industrial, public and larger residential buildings. That matters before demolition in an older building, so ask about it before walls or ceilings come down.

Insurance Notes

The Utah Insurance Department says flood insurance must be bought as a separate policy and that basic homeowners and renters policies do not cover flooding. Interior storm water is generally not covered when the roof and walls are undamaged.

That makes the cause of the water important. Photograph the water line, note where it came from and ask your insurer what your policy says.
